SECTION 4

When the Spanish arrived in Central America in the 1500s they were surprised by the variety of Indian cultures there. One of the most interesting of the cultures, the Maya, had partially collapsed hundreds of years before. There were Mayan ruins everywhere and they showed evidence of advanced engineering and a sophisticated tradition of writing. The Spanish thought that this written language could be a threat to the dominance of the Spanish language in the region. And so they tried to destroy all traces of it.

Luckily, three books written in Mayan survived the destruction and were rediscovered in the 18th and 19th century in Europe. Each of the books contained a vast collection of ancient Mayan writings. All of these books were basically handbooks to be used by the priests. But for a long time, no one could read them. In the 19th and early 20th century, investigators managed to decode some numbers and some parts of the text which seemed to be on the subject of astronomy. But there were still large parts of the text they couldn't understand.

Then in the 1950s and 1960s, there were some breakthroughs. An amateur Mayan scholar called Heinrich Berlin, working in Mexico, decoded the first Mayan glyphs. He showed that these glyphs in fact represented famous cities that had once been a part of the Mayan empire. He was then able to match these symbols with spoken words because the name for somewhere famous doesn't usually change much over the centuries.

Next to enter the picture was Yuri Norozov, a Soviet linguistics expert. He discovered that many Mayan glyphs actually played a phonetic role in the text. That means they stood for a sound, not just a single word as previously thought.

Beginning in the 1980s, several younger academics took a new approach. Unlike their forebears who studied Mayans from a distance, this new generation immersed themselves by living among the Central American indigenous people. These young researchers came to have a deeper understanding of Mayan art and also a greater appreciation of the culture of the Mayans, both past and present, as well as of their religion. All this meant that there was a dramatic improvement in the quality and quantity of translation.

The young academics discovered that many of the great events of Mayan history were recorded. In particular, many of the manuscripts described successes when kings fought wars with one another. But if the text was formal, the writing was not. The Mayan scribes who carved and painted the glyphs were often trying to be creative. So there simply wasn't a standard system. But once the young academics got familiar with the style used by a particular Mayan writer, steady translations became possible.

Starting in 2003, one prominent scholar, David Stuart, looked for an online way for scholars and amateurs to post new inscriptions and refine translations. So he started a blog that evolved into what is now the Maya Decipherment Project. Scholars in remote areas have become eager translation partners. Comments can be posted from anywhere in the world at high speed. It's estimated that there are more than 30 translators in 15 different countries currently at work.

The direction of this new understanding is that Mayans were not necessarily peaceful people who were wholly preoccupied with ceremony. This had been the general conclusion when scholarship was limited to the handbooks for priests that survived the Spanish. Thanks in part to the Maya Decipherment Project, new scholarship shows that the Mayans were interested in and wrote about the same topics as other ancient societies.
